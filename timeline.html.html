History, Trust & Your Health

Understanding the past. Navigating healthcare today. Advocating for your future.

Knowledge can help turn mistrust into informed choice.

Why talk about trust?

For generations, Black communities have experienced both extraordinary advances in medicine and profound injustices within healthcare and medical research. Medical mistrust has context. It has been shaped by documented exploitation, discrimination, lack of informed consent, unequal treatment and exclusion from the benefits of research.

EquiGen believes healthier relationships with healthcare should not require forgetting that history. They can begin with understanding it—along with knowing what protections exist today, where inequities remain and what questions you have the right to ask.

Our goal is not blind trust. It is informed trust, informed skepticism and the confidence to participate in decisions about your body, your health information and your DNA.

HeLa cells became extraordinarily important to biomedical science. Decades later, genomic sequencing of HeLa cells also raised privacy concerns for her descendants. The Belmont Report identifies three core principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. Its applications include informed consent, assessment of risks and benefits, and fair selection of research participants. Read HHS overview →

GINA protects against genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment in covered situations, but it does not generally cover life, disability or long-term-care insurance. Explore GINA at NHGRI →

What does this have to do with genetics?

DNA can reveal deeply personal information. Genetic testing and research create opportunities for prevention and discovery, but they also create reasonable questions about privacy, representation and control.

Your DNA & privacy

Ask who will receive your results, where the data will be stored, whether it may be shared for research and what privacy safeguards apply. In the U.S., GINA offers important protections in health insurance and employment, but its protections have limits, including life, disability and long-term-care insurance.

Your biological sample

Consent documents may address whether samples will be stored, how long they may be kept, whether they can be used in future research, whether data will be coded or de-identified, and who controls access. These practices vary, so asking is appropriate.

Representation affects what science can learn

When genomic datasets do not adequately represent human diversity, discoveries and some genomic tools may not perform equally well across ancestry groups. Greater representation should be paired with ethical engagement, meaningful consent, privacy safeguards and community benefit.

Your rights. Your questions. Your choice.

Choose a situation for questions you can take with you. You do not need to memorize medical terminology to ask for clarity.

Before a genetic test, consider asking:

Why are you recommending this test, and what could I learn from it?
Which genes or conditions are being tested?
What could a positive, negative or uncertain result mean for me and my family?
Who will have access to my results?
Will my sample or genetic data be stored or used for research?
Can I decline research use and still receive the clinical test?
What costs might I be responsible for?
Is genetic counseling available before or after testing?

Before joining a research study, consider asking:

What is the purpose of this study, and why am I being invited?
What will happen to me if I participate?
What are the possible risks and benefits?
What happens to my samples and data after the study ends?
Could my data or samples be used in future studies?
Who may access or share my information?
Will I receive individual results or findings?
Can I withdraw, and what happens to data already collected if I do?

At a healthcare visit, you can ask:

Can you explain that in plain language?
What are my options, including waiting or doing nothing?
What are the benefits, risks and alternatives?
Why do you recommend this option for me specifically?
Is there anything about my family history that changes this recommendation?
What should make me contact you or seek urgent care?
Can I have this information in writing so I can review it?
KNOW THE DIFFERENCE

Protections exist. Questions still matter.

Modern human-subject research includes institutional review and informed-consent requirements, and the Belmont principles remain foundational to research ethics. Genomic research also has specific consent and data-sharing considerations.

Those protections are meaningful—but they do not require you to surrender your judgment. A consent form should support a decision, not replace a conversation.

Genetic privacy note: GINA is important, but it is not a universal genetic-privacy law. Before testing, it can be useful to understand how genetic information may interact with different types of insurance and applicable state law.

Trust should be earned through trustworthiness.

Knowing this history does not require rejecting medicine, research or genetic testing. It gives us context for asking better questions and expecting healthcare and research institutions to earn trust through transparency, respect, accountability, equitable treatment and meaningful partnership with the communities they serve.
